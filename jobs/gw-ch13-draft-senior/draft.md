*Friday, January 29, 2027, 8:10 a.m.*

The support queue was on Derek's left monitor. On the right, his proposal had a question and no answer under *Cost*.

He sat with the notebook beside the keyboard, open to the old benchmark settings. Tolliver wanted the proposal by nine. The correction had already gone; this document was supposed to say what Derek would do next.

Priya's message appeared in Teams on the docked laptop.

*Available for a check before you send?*

He replied that he could join at 8:15. Until then he stayed in the queue. When the time came, he lifted the headset from the desk, opened their arranged video call and enabled his microphone and camera. Priya's video appeared. He selected the proposal window in the sharing controls and started sharing it.

“Startup, then a fixed diagnostic,” he said. “Same settings as the recorded runs. I want to compare what happens now.”

“Compare which part?”

“Loading. Then a small set of the benchmark questions.”

“And if the score's different?”

He looked down at the two dated tables in the notebook.

“I'd know we hadn't reproduced the earlier result.”

“You wouldn't have reproduced the earlier test. You'd have run a sample.”

“Right.”

“Do you know what compiled state survived the maintenance?”

“No. The stored file matched when I checked it Monday. I haven't run it.”

“Then don't promise to explain the score change with this.”

The proposal's question still said *Reproduce January 14th and January 16th results*. He had put both dates there because leaving either out seemed like conceding something.

He selected the sentence and replaced it.

*Can the retained copy load using the recorded configuration, and what startup and diagnostic behavior can we record?*

Priya read it.

“That's a first test,” she said.

“It doesn't answer why the numbers changed.”

“No.”

He took the pen from beside the notebook and wrote *startup first* below the settings. The old scores stayed on the page. He would use the settings as a reference, not describe the notebook as a current control.

“I want the company to pay for that,” he said. “Characterize the file it's retaining. One job, limited time. Keep the output even if it fails to load.”

“Where does the record go?”

“Company records. You get access to it.”

“Separately from what you send me afterward.”

“Yes. Commands and writes too. If we can't record those, we don't start. If recording fails, we stop.”

“And no extra job while you're fixing the first one.”

He added that to the proposal.

With the share still limited to the document, he opened the reservation interface on the other monitor and chose the estimate view. Two GPUs, two hours. He entered the duration and requested the preview.

The all-in estimate was twelve dollars. Four allocated GPU-hours. The booking button was below it.

He copied the quoted ceiling into the proposal and left the button alone.

“Two cards for two hours,” he said. “No extension if loading takes the whole window.”

“What happens then?”

“I report that it didn't get to the diagnostic.”

Priya was quiet long enough for him to look at her video.

“I'll review that request,” she said. “You haven't got my agreement to run it.”

“I know.”

He stopped sharing, said goodbye and left the call. The headset went back on the desk. He completed the proposal with the retained path, the saved loader, a fixed diagnostic sample and the recording and stop requirements they had discussed. Under payment, he entered the requested company support allocation.

At 8:44 a.m. he attached the document to a new company email addressed to Tolliver, copied Priya and sent it. The sent item contained the proposal. Sixteen minutes remained before the deadline.

In a separate message to Tolliver, he asked for available transfer and home-endpoint records from his two failed attempts to be preserved through the appropriate owner. He had deleted the landed files. He did not know what records remained, and he asked for no new transfer.

At 8:52 a.m. a meeting invitation arrived for 11:10 with Tolliver and Priya. Derek opened it and accepted.

There was still no answer to the proposal. He brought the queue forward and opened the next ticket.

*Friday, January 29, 2027, 11:10 a.m.*

Derek was seated at the office desk when he opened the accepted invitation. He put on the headset, joined Teams and enabled his microphone and camera. Tolliver and Priya appeared in separate video tiles.

He selected the proposal window and started sharing.

“I want permission for one supervised job,” he said. “Company support allocation, twelve-dollar ceiling. I load the retained copy and run the diagnostic if there's time. Priya gets the record independently.”

Tolliver had the proposal in front of him.

“Why should the company pay for your investigation?”

“To find out what we're retaining. Whether it starts, what resources it uses, what it writes.”

“You wanted to explain the benchmark scores yesterday.”

“I still do. This won't necessarily explain them.”

Derek stopped moving the pointer. The question at the top of the document was the smaller one he and Priya had left there.

“If it fails to load, you still get the startup record,” he said. “If it runs, you get the commands, output and writes. The allocation and times come from the job record. I don't just send you my account of it.”

“What would that let us decide?”

“Whether another test can be specified. Whether keeping it available means keeping something we can actually operate.”

Tolliver looked toward his screen.

“And it might tell us the next request isn't worth approving.”

“Yes.”

Derek had been imagining a successful diagnostic when he wrote the request. Tolliver was buying the failed one too.

“Priya?”

“I'll witness this job,” she said. “Provided I can inspect the record without Derek selecting it for me, and I can require an abort. That's not sponsorship of further work.”

“I'll stop when you ask,” Derek said.

“The twelve dollars isn't the whole cost,” Tolliver said. “Both of you have work. I don't want two hours turning into the rest of Monday.”

“Ten until noon on February 1st,” Derek said. “Hard limit on the job. Two GPUs at most. Four GPU-hours. If it hasn't loaded by then, that's the result.”

He moved the pointer to the allowance.

“The quote includes what you've specified here?”

“Yes. I haven't booked it.”

“Keep it that way until access and availability are confirmed. Use the normal service request for the permissions. Whoever controls the resource has to confirm the setup; this call doesn't do that.”

Derek took the pen from beside the notebook.

“And I need a place for runtime files,” he said. “Separate from the weights. Startup may have to compile again. Keeping the source read-only can't mean nothing is allowed to write anywhere.”

Priya nodded.

“Specify the working area. Collect its writes. No customer inputs and no network tasks.”

“If the record can't show the commands and files, we don't start,” he said. “A start and end time won't do it.”

“And test the abort before the job,” she said.

He wrote that below the ceiling.

“What if it writes another script?” Tolliver asked.

“Keep it as output,” Derek said. “Don't execute it until Priya has looked at the exact file and checked that it's still the diagnostic we've agreed to.”

“That doesn't let me approve a different task,” Priya said.

“No training. No child jobs,” Derek said. “No extra resources.”

Tolliver looked back at the camera.

“If the job loses recording, or tries to change the source or run outside those limits?”

“I stop it. Keep the record of what happened.”

“You don't fix it and start another.”

“No. I'd ask again.”

Derek had written *one job* in the notebook. He added *including failure*. A loading error would not leave him an unused permission to spend on a replacement.

Tolliver said he would authorize the support allocation for this characterization. The four QA charges stayed under separate review. This job was to use its actual purpose, not the old ticket reference.

Derek left the correction closed.

“If we get through the diagnostic,” he said, “I'd like to bring you a comparison request afterward.”

“You can bring a request. You won't have permission for it from this one.”

“All right.”

“And if the next request is your own research, don't assume this allocation follows you.”

“I haven't funded my personal code.”

“I know.”

There was room below the stop conditions for another test. Derek did not add one.

Tolliver began writing his reply on the proposal thread. Derek stopped sharing so the manager could work from his own copy. Priya stayed on the call.

“I need the record even if there's nothing interesting in it,” she said.

“You'll have it.”

“I mean an ordinary failure too. Not only one you think needs explaining.”

“Yes.”

The email notification arrived on Derek's laptop at 11:43 a.m. He brought the company client forward and opened Tolliver's reply. Priya was copied.

*Approved for one characterization job on Monday, February 1st, 10 a.m. to noon local time, using /ns/dvoss-isolated/v9 as a read-only source on Meridian. Maximum two GPUs, four allocated GPU-hours and the quoted $12 ceiling, charged to the company support allocation under my approval. Priya must witness the job and have independent access to its records.*

*Confirm resource-owner access, availability, a separate approved working area, independent recording and effective abort/time limits before booking or launch. Retain the job allocation and start/end records, output and errors, commands, working-area writes and generated scripts. Use the saved loader and the specified diagnostic only. A new script may be retained as output; execution requires Priya's inspection of the exact file for that scope. No training, child jobs, network tasks, customer inputs or extra resources.*

*Stop for loss of recording, out-of-scope execution or writes, a source-modification attempt, Priya's abort request or any ceiling reached. Preserve the failure record and copy results to Priya and me. No retry or second session without another permission. The existing hold remains except for this job and its recorded working-area writes: no source moves, deletion or external circulation. The past allocation correction remains a separate decision.*

Derek read the window and allowance once more. There was no permission to run that afternoon, or to use Monday's unused minutes on Tuesday.

“Those are the terms I'm accepting,” he said.

He had one more question before sending the reply.

“I need to tell my roommate I've been involved. He's heard about the public release. I've been letting him think I was just reading about it.”

“What are you asking to show him?” Tolliver said.

“Nothing. My own part in it. The time, the unauthorized compute. That I may borrow money if I keep going.”

“You can tell him about your own conduct and spending,” Tolliver said. “Keep the artifact, company records, private measurements, paths and customer details out of it. This doesn't authorize circulating evidence.”

“I understand.”

“And your preservation request,” Tolliver said. “I've routed it to the appropriate owner. I don't have confirmation that those endpoint records were retained.”

Derek wrote *preservation pending* in the notebook. He had asked for someone to look; he did not know whether there was anything left to preserve.

At 11:47 a.m. he sent his acceptance on the thread. Then he said goodbye, left the meeting and put the headset on the desk.

The Monday window was in the received email. It was not on the job board.

*Friday, January 29, 2027, 6:40 p.m.*

Derek came from the office and stopped at the kitchen counter. Theo was eating at the secondhand table. A pot of rice stood on the stove with the serving spoon beside it.

“I'm in early tomorrow,” Theo said. “If you're planning one of those nights.”

“I'll keep it quiet.”

“Quiet includes talking at the computer.”

“I know.”

Derek took a bowl from the cupboard and a spoon from the drawer. He served rice from the pot, returned the serving spoon beside it, and carried his bowl and spoon to the table. The bowl was warm against his palm. He set it down, pulled out a chair and sat.

The notebook and laptop were still in the office. He had left them there.

“I need to tell you about the model,” he said. “The one we talked about when the public download came out.”

Theo looked up.

“I was testing a private file before that. On the company systems. It isn't a file I got from the public download, and I don't know how they're related.”

“How long have you been doing that?”

“Since before I told you about the release.”

“So when I asked you about checking it?”

“I told you how to check a download. I didn't tell you what I'd been doing.”

Theo put his spoon in his bowl.

“Okay.”

“I used company compute without approval. Charged it as testing for work when it was my own investigation.”

Derek had not eaten any rice. He took a mouthful because it was there, then had to swallow before he could finish.

“I've given them the account of it. The charges are still being reviewed.”

“Do you still have a job?”

“Yes.”

“Are they making you pay?”

“They haven't decided what happens to those charges.”

Theo waited.

“The file is still on their systems,” Derek said. “They're retaining it. I don't have a usable copy here.”

“And you've been trying to get one?”

“Yes. I shouldn't have left that out either.”

He could explain the failed transfers, and then they would be talking about files instead of why he had let Theo ask questions without answering them. He stopped there.

“I have permission for a test on Monday,” he said. “The company pays for that one. Two hours, supervised. It might only tell us whether we can get it running again.”

“That's what you arranged today?”

“Yes.”

“Then what are you telling me before you arrange it?”

Derek set his spoon down beside the bowl.

“The rest. If I keep working on it, I may need to borrow money.”

Theo leaned back far enough to look at him across the table.

“Have you already borrowed it?”

“No.”

“I haven't settled an amount or payments,” Derek said. “I've been thinking about it.”

“For computer time?”

“For more testing. Monday isn't permission for that, and the company isn't promising to pay for it.”

“You used their computers without asking, and now you want to pay to use computers.”

Derek almost said that it was more complicated.

“Yes.”

Theo picked up his spoon again, but did not eat.

“I can see why you started with the good news.”

“The good news was that I was allowed to test it.”

“I meant the part where you still have a job.”

Derek looked at his bowl. He had thought getting permission would make this conversation easier. He was sitting at dinner telling Theo he had something to lose that Theo had not known was at risk.

“I'm not asking you to borrow anything,” he said. “Or cover my share.”

“Then don't make a plan that needs me to.”

“I won't.”

“Rent and utilities. Your share still comes when it's due. I don't want to hear that a test went over or the loan payment was bigger than you thought.”

“I haven't taken a loan.”

“I heard you. I'm telling you before you do.”

Derek nodded.

“And I have to sleep,” Theo said. “I'm already going in early on Saturday. I don't want to come home to you deciding this is the night you have to talk to a machine until two.”

“I said I'd keep it quiet.”

“Tonight. I'm talking about the next time it's important.”

Derek reached for his spoon, then left it where it was.

He had been ready to promise quiet as if that would settle the money too. Theo had asked about two different things.

“I'll keep the late calls and tests out of your work nights,” he said. “If there's something I can't schedule that way, I'll talk to you before I commit to it.”

“Not after.”

“Before.”

Theo ate a spoonful of rice.

“Why didn't you tell me?”

“I didn't know what I'd found.”

“You still don't.”

“No.”

“That didn't stop you telling me about the bill.”

Derek could remember that conversation well enough to know Theo was right. Back then, the questions had seemed harmless to bring into the kitchen. Later he had started giving Theo the version without his part in it.

“I didn't want you asking whether I should be doing it,” he said. “I wanted to keep going.”

Theo looked at him.

“I had reasons for not telling people at work too,” Derek said. “Some were about keeping them out of trouble. But not telling them meant I didn't have to answer. I was doing that here.”

“You could have just said you didn't want my opinion.”

“I would've got it anyway.”

“Probably.”

Derek picked up his spoon and tried another mouthful. Theo ate too. Neither of them had to find something encouraging to say about the model.

After a while Theo asked, “What if the two hours aren't enough?”

“I stop. That's part of the permission.”

“And then?”

“I look at what we got. If I want more, I have to ask again and work out how to pay.”

“What if you get just enough to want more every time?”

Derek lowered his spoon into the bowl. He had been treating Monday as the test that would let him specify the next one. Theo was asking how that ever ended.

“I don't know yet.”

“You need to know what you can spend even if you don't know that.”

“Yes.”

Theo finished the rice on his spoon.

“So what can you spend?”

Derek stayed in the chair. There was more rice in his bowl, and he had not answered the question.

“I haven't worked it out,” he said. “Not after rent and bills.”
